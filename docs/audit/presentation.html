The bottom line

Real engineering.
Real problem. Wrong lock
on the front door.

You have built something genuinely hard — a system that predicts a laptop is going to fail and fixes it automatically, before anyone raises a ticket. Very few companies have that working. You have it working.

But the same system currently lets a single help-desk login run any command on every company laptop — and the safety check that was meant to prevent that can be walked straight past.

Not investable today. Investable after one focused week of fixes — because the hard part is already done.

What the product actually is

Two products in one,
which is the good idea.

Half one
The service desk
Where employees raise IT problems and staff resolve them. Tickets, knowledge articles, service requests, response-time targets. This is a crowded, mature market.
Half two
The eyes on every laptop
A small program on each company device reporting how it is really performing — speed, memory, disk, battery, crashes, network. This is the valuable, less crowded half.

The thesis is right. Today companies buy these separately, pay twice, and still cannot connect "this employee is frustrated" to "this laptop is dying." Joining them is a real, expensive, unsolved problem — and joining them is exactly what you have done.

Start with what is genuinely good

Three things here are
better than the funded
competition.

Strength 01
It predicts failure, and shows its working
Most products give you a mysterious "risk score." Yours shows the actual trend, how confident it is, and how many days until the disk fills up — and it refuses to guess when the data is too thin. In a market full of unprovable AI claims, that honesty is a genuine selling point.
Strength 02
The full loop actually works
Predict a problem → raise a ticket → fix the machine → close the ticket, with no human involved. Competitors have one half or the other. Nobody has the whole loop at your price point.
Strength 03
The AI assistant cannot be tricked into leaking data
It is built so the AI physically cannot ask for another person's information — there is no way to phrase the request. Most companies get this wrong. This is the single best piece of judgement in the codebase.

This matters because it proves the team can build carefully. The problems ahead are not a talent gap.

The one finding that outweighs everything else

Anyone on the help desk
can run any command on
every company laptop.

Your product can send instructions to every device it monitors — that is the "fix it automatically" feature, and it is supposed to be a strength.

You built a safety check: an AI reviews each instruction and refuses dangerous ones. But that check lives in a separate door. Someone can skip it entirely and send instructions straight to every laptop.

Think of it as a master key that opens every laptop in the company — issued to every help-desk account, including contractors and temporary staff. The guard who was supposed to check the key is standing at a different entrance.

For a customer that manages IT for other companies, this reaches every one of their clients too. In a bank or a hospital this is a reportable security incident, not a bug.

Four more doors left unlocked

Critical
Four back-office areas have no lock at all
Company equipment records, response-time policies, and the knowledge library can be changed by any logged-in user. An employee can delete the very policies that govern how fast their own tickets must be answered.
Critical
One customer can edit another customer's data
Six places check who you are but forget to check which company you belong to. In multi-customer software this single issue ends enterprise deals on the spot.
Critical
When identity checks fail, everyone lands in the same room
If the login system hiccups, the software quietly puts that person into a shared space instead of stopping them. Failures should close the door, not open a different one.
Critical
The laptop program is unsigned
Nothing proves the software came from you. Corporate antivirus will flag it, staff will see scary warnings, and nobody can tell your program apart from a tampered copy of it.

All four are days of work, not months.

What large organisations require before they will even trial software

Ten gates. You pass none
of them today.

  • ✕Single sign-on — staff log in with their existing company account. Asked on every enterprise form.
  • ✕Automatic account removal — when HR terminates someone, access must drop instantly.
  • ✕SOC 2 certification — the independent security audit. Requested before any technical evaluation.
  • ✕Change management — proof of who approved each modification. An audit requirement, not a feature preference.
  • ✕Mac support — typically 20–40% of a modern company's laptops.
  • ✕Signed software — cryptographic proof the program is really yours.
  • ✕Disaster recovery — today there is no reliable way to rebuild the database if it is lost.
  • ✕Accessibility standards — legally required for government buyers. You named government as a market.
  • ✕Works on a phone — the employee portal is unusable on mobile at any screen size.
  • ✕Referenceable customers — buyers ask to call two. There is no answer to that yet.

Every monitored laptop writes a status record every 30 seconds — 2,880 records per laptop per day — and nothing ever deletes them.

At a thousand-person customer the database is storing over a billion records a year while simultaneously trying to power a dashboard that refreshes every fifteen seconds. It will not cope.

The fix is ordinary and well understood: keep fine detail for a week, keep summaries for two years, delete the rest. Roughly two weeks of work — and it also turns storage cost from a runaway expense into a flat one.

Positioning

Stop trying to beat
ServiceNow. You will lose
that argument.

Do not do this
"A cheaper ServiceNow"
You lose on every feature checklist — the comparison table decides it before anyone sees a demo. Replacing ServiceNow takes companies 18–24 months, costs millions, and puts the sponsor's career at risk. This is how service-desk startups die.
Do this instead
"We fix problems before employees notice"
Sell the monitoring and prediction to companies that already have a service desk. Prove value in 30 days with real predicted failures. Replace their service desk later, once you are already useful to them.

Your realistic first customer today: a 150–800 person technology company with a small IT team, mostly Windows laptops, already paying for a service desk that cannot tell them why machines are slow — and not yet forced to demand security certifications.

The only advantage competitors cannot buy

You can see across every
customer at once.
Nobody else can.

Because all customers run on one shared system, you can answer questions no single-company tool ever can:

"Your laptops start up slower than 60% of manufacturers your size."
Every competitor sees one company. You see the whole population.
"This driver version crashes three times more often — across 40,000 machines."
Advice no individual customer could ever work out alone.

This gets better with every customer you add and cannot be replicated by a competitor with more money — they would need the customers first. It is the one genuine moat available to you.

One cheap thing to start now: record whether each prediction came true. Today the system deletes them and forgets. Keeping that history costs days and is the raw material for a prediction engine nobody can copy.

The good news

The most dangerous
problems are one week
of work.

  • 01Remove the master key. The laptop program should only accept a fixed list of safe, named actions — never free-form instructions.
  • 02Put locks on the four unlocked areas. Equipment records, response policies, workflows, knowledge library.
  • 03Check which company, not just which person. Six places, one line each.
  • 04Make failed logins stop, not redirect. Close the door instead of opening a shared one.
  • 05Take the invented claims off the website. Hours of work. Removes a legal exposure entirely.
  • 06Stop writing fake data into real customer accounts. New customers currently see invented security information on first login.

After this week, the honest answer to "is it safe to install?" changes from no to yes. That is the highest-value week of engineering available anywhere in this business.

Closing

The distance to a real
business is shorter than
the scores suggest.

The genuinely difficult thing — predicting a failure from real data and fixing it automatically — is already built and working. That is the part that is hard to copy and hard to buy.

Certifications, single sign-on and change management are expensive and slow, but no competent team fails at them. They are known quantities with known timelines.

What concerns me is not the missing features. It is that software which runs commands on employee laptops shipped without a lock, and that the website makes claims the product contradicts. Both are symptoms of moving fast without a review step — and both are habits, not talent problems.

Fix the week-one list. Then build the benchmarking advantage, because it is the only thing here that a better-funded competitor cannot simply outspend you to replicate.
